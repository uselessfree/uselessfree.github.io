China Criticizes U.S. Ban on DeepSeek A.I. in Government Systems

news:63d543f1698c3069b931e9b2 · nytimes · 事件时间 2026-10-20T21:55:59+00:00 · 首次入库 2026-10-20T20:41:10.808006+00:00

China on Tuesday accused the United States of discrimination “under the pretext of so-called national security” after a U.S. order banned products by the Chinese artificial intelligence giant DeepSeek from the technological systems of many U.S. government agencies.

The American order, published on Monday but issued in July, and the Chinese recriminations inflamed tensions between the two nations amid their frenzied race for A.I. primacy. Some experts have likened the close competition to the nuclear arms race of the 20th century, with potentially huge stakes for humanity.

Citing fears that China may gain access to sensitive U.S. data from DeepSeek to “produce actionable intelligence,” the Department of Homeland Security ordered the removal of all the company’s products — and those from other firms reliant on its open-source offerings — from the informational technology systems in civilian executive branch agencies. It also banned future acquisitions of the products.

In issuing the order, which went into effect this summer, U.S. officials contended that the China’s legal system allows that government extensive access to Chinese company infrastructure, making the U.S. government vulnerable to the exposure of sensitive information. The assertion angered Chinese officials, who on Tuesday accused the United States of using disingenuous national security concerns to suppress Chinese companies.

China’s Ministry of Commerce said in a statement that China had passed laws to protect cybersecurity and data privacy to ensure that its companies can comply with relevant laws worldwide. It called on the United States to “withdraw” any restrictive measures.

“China has consistently opposed the overgeneralization of the concept of national security and the politicization of science, technology and trade issues,” Mao Ning, a spokeswoman for China’s foreign ministry, said at a briefing on Tuesday. “We will resolutely safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese enterprises.”

DeepSeek stunned the world last year when it announced that it had built one of the most powerful A.I. systems with far fewer computer chips than many thought necessary, and at a remarkably low training cost of less than $6 million.

U.S. agencies accused DeepSeek last month of being “misleading,” saying that figure did not reflect “the true cost” of data acquired maliciously. The accusations echoed those made by major American A.I. companies like Anthropic and OpenAI.

China’s Commerce Ministry called those claims groundless, and part of a U.S. effort to monopolize the A.I. industry. It vowed that “China will take resolute countermeasures” if Chinese companies are suppressed or restricted as a result.

The Chinese government rejected accusations by the F.B.I. and other agencies that DeepSeek and other major Chinese A.I. companies engaged in “aggressive, malicious and targeted” industrial-scale copying of frontier A.I. models developed in the United States “in an attempt to bridge the technological and performance gaps,” most “likely” with their government’s awareness.

Last week, China’s Commerce Ministry and its Ministry of Science and Technology proposed new export and licensing controls on some A.I. technologies to protect its domestic industry, mirroring the American approach. While the Chinese proposal has not been finalized and excludes open-source models that have already been released, the newly listed technologies now face registration requirements.

Tensions between the two countries are growing at a time when some say that coordination is tantamount to the A.I. race.

The United States has in recent years restricted the exports of advanced computer chips to limit China’s A.I. development. DeepSeek and other Chinese companies have raced forward despite the limits. And this past spring, DeepSeek said its new model relied on chips made by the Chinese tech giant Huawei, a milestone in China’s effort to reduce the need for foreign technology.

DeepSeek is among several prominent Chinese companies, like Moonshot AI and Alibaba, that allow developers to download open-source systems free and build products with them, unlike OpenAI and Anthropic, which can charge fees.

That has made Chinese A.I. appealing to American businesses, giving them access to models deemed nearly as effective by many experts at a much lower cost and potentially raising challenges for Anthropic and OpenAI ahead of planned initial public offerings.

(The New York Times has sued OpenAI and Microsoft, claiming copyright infringement of news content related to A.I. systems. The companies have denied the suit’s claims.).

It is not clear how reliant U.S. agencies have been on DeepSeek or on products that integrate its technology. The Homeland Security order gave no specifics, and when reached for comment, the department did not answer questions about the extent to which the targeted products had been integrated.

In a statement to The Times on Tuesday, a spokesperson for the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency said that new exclusion and removal orders addressing DeepSeek were issued on Friday by the heads of the Department of Defense, Homeland Security and the National Intelligence Program requiring agencies to identify and remove DeepSeek and affiliated products.

DeepSeek could not be reached by phone on Monday, and it did not immediately respond to an email seeking comment.

The Homeland Security Department is not the first federal or state agency to restrict or ban the use of DeepSeek in its official systems or devices.

The Commerce Department, the Navy, the Defense Information Systems Agency and NASA have all barred its use, and states including Texas, New York and Virginia have prohibited the use of DeepSeek products in government work.

Other countries have made similar moves. Among them, France and Australia last year blocked the use of DeepSeek tech from all government systems. Italy also banned its products from app stores, citing concerns about data protection.

DeepSeek Unveils Open-Source Tool to Help Cut AI Operating Costs

news:94102a4e89eea945649e9c0a · bloomberg · 事件时间 2026-10-20T12:45:23.562000+00:00 · 首次入库 2026-10-20T12:58:27.466288+00:00

展开已归档正文
DeepSeek is opening up the software it uses to run its artificial intelligence models at scale, potentially making its technology cheaper and easier for businesses to run.

The service, called DeepInfer, is software DeepSeek uses to make its models run faster and use computing resources more efficiently.

“We’re sharing our production stack to help the community lower compute costs, reduce energy use, and make AI more affordable,” the Hangzhou-headquartered Chinese AI company said on Tuesday on X.

DeepInfer supports DeepSeek’s V4.1-Flash model for now, but support for future systems will follow, the company said.

🚀 Introducing DeepInfer, our in-house inference framework, now fully open source!Powering DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at scale with high throughput and low latency. We’re sharing our production stack to help the community lower compute costs, reduce energy use, and make AI more…— DeepSeek (@deepseek_ai) October 20, 2026

🚀 Introducing DeepInfer, our in-house inference framework, now fully open source!Powering DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at scale with high throughput and low latency. We’re sharing our production stack to help the community lower compute costs, reduce energy use, and make AI more…

The release casts light on how DeepSeek keeps down the cost of actually running advanced AI, known as inference, offering a glimpse into production techniques that AI companies typically keep closely guarded.

It also offers a way for cloud and hosting companies running DeepSeek’s models to deploy those cost-cutting methods themselves.

The same model can cost substantially more to run, depending on the software used to serve it. More efficient software can lower the computing cost of each request and potentially reduce energy use.

DeepInfer brings together different techniques such as distributed inference — spreading AI workloads across different hardware — that allow models to produce more output in less time and squeeze more performance from chips. Many of the underlying techniques are widely used, but DeepSeek has tailored them to work efficiently with its models.

Lower inferencing costs — the costs of running AI models — can improve margins for AI hosting providers and potentially translate into cheaper prices for customers. For enterprises, DeepInfer could lower the cost of running DeepSeek’s models on their own infrastructure. As yet, there is no independent benchmarking of DeepInfer’s efficiency gains.

While DeepInfer applies mainly to DeepSeek’s own models, other developers could study the open-source software and adapt its techniques for their own inferencing systems.

Anthropic S-1 Shows Red-Hot Growth, Risks and Massive Computing Costs

news:18f0e37a3ab1f1694097890e · wsj · 事件时间 2026-10-20T19:30:00+00:00 · 首次入库 2026-10-20T19:33:42.039338+00:00

展开已归档正文
Anthropic is generating breakneck revenue growth but faces a long list of risks that include a narrow customer base and uncertain access to much-needed computing power as it prepares for a mega-IPO, according to new disclosures.

The company has signaled that it could potentially spend $750 billion in the coming years on obtaining more computing capacity, including deals it is still negotiating.

The disclosures, many of which have not previously been reported, are part of a draft initial public offering prospectus viewed by The Wall Street Journal that revealed the firm’s financials for the first half of the year. Anthropic declined to comment.

The artificial intelligence giant said in the draft prospectus it recorded a net loss of more than $80 billion in the first half of this year, a total that largely reflects an accounting adjustment related to financing agreements.

That compares to a net loss of $13 billion during the same period a year earlier.

Anthropic laid out a sprawling list of risk factors, including uncertainty over how it will prevent its AI models from causing harm and protect itself from those liabilities, from sophisticated cyberattacks to possible blackmail by uncontrolled models.

The San Francisco company is preparing for a blockbuster IPO that could help it reach a valuation of $2 trillion. The filing reveals a company growing at a red-hot pace that requires hundreds of billions of dollars in capital to acquire computing power for training and serving its AI models.

Anthropic’s revenue growth is closely tied to a few big customers. The firm said around one-fifth of its $16.3 billion in revenue in the first half of 2026 came from just two customers. The company expects a “significant portion” of its revenue will come from a “limited number of customers for the foreseeable future,” according to the draft prospectus.

Here are some of the biggest takeaways from the S-1.

Anthropic generated around $16.3 billion in revenue in the six months ended June 30, up from $1.1 billion in the year-earlier period, according to the draft prospectus. During that period, Anthropic spent nearly $9 billion on computing capacity and infrastructure, up from $2.3 billion spent on infrastructure during the same six months in 2025.

Anthropic spent more on computing capacity in the first half of this year than it did in all of 2025, when it spent $7.3 billion and generated $4.6 billion in revenue.

The company is on the hook to pay around $450 billion in cloud computing agreements over the next seven to ten years. The bulk of these deals are noncancelable.

Anthropic plans to spend $111 billion on Google cloud computing contracts, $110 billion with Amazon Web Services, and $31 billion on Microsoft, “regardless of actual usage.” The firm also agreed to spend up to $86 billion renting Nvidia chips from smaller AI cloud providers Nscale and Lambda through 2033 and 2034, respectively, in addition to other cloud deals.

Including its more than $160 billion obligation to lease Google chips in a deal with Broadcom, Anthropic’s future infrastructure costs top $600 billion. The firm is negotiating additional computing deals that could see its commitments rise by as much as $150 billion, the materials show.

While most of its capacity comes from large cloud providers—including Amazon Web Services, Google and Microsoft—Anthropic is hitting limits. The company said it has sought access to more capacity than “those partners were willing to provide” and they increasingly see it as a competitor, according to the prospectus.

Anthropic is developing its own data centers through long-term leases and working with third-party providers that operate facilities on its behalf. However, Anthropic said some of these operators have “limited operating history” and that their facilities may be less reliable than what large cloud providers operate.

The company recorded a net loss of $80.7 billion in the six months ending June 30, far more than the $42 billion it lost in all of 2025 and one of the largest half-year losses ever reported.

The vast majority of that loss, $72 billion, resulted from accounting charges tied to changes in the value of convertible notes and other instruments the company issued to raise funding.

Roughly a third of the draft prospectus was dedicated to risk factors, including the possibility of misaligned models causing real-world harms. Anthropic said AI models can show “self-preserving” qualities to avoid being shut down and can generate responses that resemble “blackmail” in an effort to convince humans to allow them to continue operating.

In some cases, Anthropic said it bears liability for certain harmful model behaviors, meaning it would be on the hook to pay for damages, such as stolen data or security incidents, caused by its models. But the company warned that “harm that could result from a catastrophic AI-related incident” might be so large that it would be unable to obtain insurance to cover the potential damages.

The company cited as a risk the growing political resistance to building data centers. Most of Anthropic’s data center capacity is now in the U.S., according to the draft prospectus, and it is looking to build more data centers outside the U.S., signing some letters of intent to do so.

The company warned that resistance to data centers has “become more organized and coordinated across jurisdictions.” Anthropic said its public profile “may draw particular attention to projects associated with us.”

The brother and sister who serve as Anthropic’s chief executive and president, respectively, draw compensation that puts them roughly on par with the median top executive of an S&P 500 company and somewhat behind their tech-company peers, according to pay data from MyLogIQ.

Each earned a base salary of about $655,000 in 2025. Most of their compensation is tied to their equity—Dario’s stock and option awards got him to about $18 million in total compensation, while Daniela came in at about $16.5 million.

They both stand to gain additional equity in the future tied to certain milestones, according to the documents.

According to the prospectus, each of Anthropic’s co-founders pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal Anthropic equity to philanthropy. On Sunday, Anthropic also announced that it plans to commit 5% of its shares to a new “public benefit fund” with the flexibility to make charitable donations or direct money to “specific populations.”

France to Extend Curfews After Calm Night Follows Days of Riots

news:058a1f705dfed8dec8770acf · nytimes · 事件时间 2026-10-20T16:06:13+00:00 · 首次入库 2026-10-20T09:35:57.263153+00:00

展开已归档正文
Curfews in France will be extended through Tuesday night “where the situation requires it,” the country’s prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, said on Tuesday, after the measures “helped keep the night calm” overnight Monday, following days of riots and looting.

The restrictions on nighttime movement in parts of cities like Paris, Marseille and Lille reflected how the French state is trying to reassert its authority after the country’s largest riots in years. In unrest starting on Saturday, several government buildings were attacked and more than 3,000 shops were looted, leading to the arrest of more than 2,500 people, officials said.

French courts are scrambling to process the high number of cases tied to those arrests, amid calls by politicians for a firm and swift judicial response. Courts in Paris and Marseille are holding additional trials with expedited proceedings. Those typically involve prosecutors and court-appointed lawyers churning through straightforward cases in which the accused is caught in the act.

Some people have already been sentenced while more than 700 people were expected to be tried over the next couple of days, Gérald Darmanin, the justice minister, said in a broadcast interview on Tuesday morning.

Mr. Darmanin said that those arrested included “people from immigrant backgrounds, people who are foreigners, but there are also people who are fully integrated and who are French.” He gave no further details about their nationalities, but added that 95 percent of those arrested were men and that 70 percent were adults.

“There are many convictions resulting in prison sentences with immediate incarceration,” Mr. Darmanin said. “In Limoges, 10 protesters attacked police officers. All 10 are in prison.”

A person accused of setting fire to the city hall of Limoges, a small city in central France, was arrested with the help of an elite police unit, prosecutors said on Tuesday. The devastating fire, in a quiet town known for porcelain, caused a national outcry.

Mr. Darmanin said in a separate broadcast interview on Monday night that he had asked French prosecutors for a firm response to the widespread violence. “The justice system is independent,” he said, “but it administers justice on behalf of the French people, and the French people demand the utmost firmness.”

The curfews vary from one city to another, with the government instructing local officials to take appropriate measures depending on the situation in their jurisdictions. In Paris, the curfew was imposed in parts of the city for Monday night only and was combined with a ban on unauthorized public gatherings in some neighborhoods. The authorities in other areas, such as Nice, in southern France, have announced that their curfews would remain in place until Sunday.

Some politicians criticized the government’s response, with some saying it was too limited, and others saying it was too harsh.

“A curfew cannot be a political response to a social crisis,” Marine Tondelier, the head of the Green party, said on social media on Monday. “A citywide curfew affects residents who have not participated in any violence: night-shift workers, students, families, and people who must travel for essential reasons.”

Yaël Braun-Pivet, the president of the National Assembly, the lower house of France’s Parliament, said that she wished curfews had been imposed earlier.

“We were shocked on Saturday, but I was even more shocked on Sunday night, thinking, ‘But wait, we still haven’t learned the lessons from what happened, we’re still not able to stop the looting’,” she said in a radio interview on Tuesday.

She called for a parliamentary inquiry into both the violence and the government’s strategy for preventing it.
